START HERE

A delivery date is
a promise to a customer.

Imagine joining a marketplace’s operations meeting for the first time. Sellers send products through logistics partners. Customers are given an estimated arrival date. The team needs to know where to focus when that date is missed.

Where are orders losing time, and what should the business investigate to make delivery more reliable?

I use Olist’s real, anonymised order records to connect delivery performance with customer experience, then translate the findings into a proposed operations review and exception queue.

This is an independent portfolio case study using public historical data, not work commissioned by Olist or a description of its current performance.

THE 30-SECOND ANSWER

Investigate the journey.
Don’t lose sight of the customer.

Of 96.5k eligible delivered orders, 6.8% arrived late. Low ratings appeared on 62.4% of reviewed late orders, versus 9.3% of reviewed on-time orders. The longest elapsed stage for late orders was after carrier handover. That is a starting point for investigation, not proof of carrier fault.

THE PROCESS, IN PLAIN LANGUAGE

  1. 01 / PurchaseThe customer places an order.
  2. 02 / ApprovalThe order is approved.
  3. 03 / HandoverThe carrier receives it.
  4. 04 / DeliveryIt reaches the customer.
What does “late” mean here?

Delivery occurred after the estimated calendar date. An arrival during the promised day counts as on time. A long journey can still be on time if the promise allowed for it.

The main rate covers delivered orders with valid core dates. Cancelled, shipped and other non-delivered records are a separate population, not successful deliveries.

01 / WHY THE CUSTOMER MATTERS

Late delivery comes with a different review profile.

Nearly two in three reviewed late orders received only one or two stars. For on-time orders, it was fewer than one in ten.

What this changes

Delivery exceptions deserve a customer-support response as well as an operations review. A proactive-contact pilot is worth considering.

Ratings cover the whole purchase experience. This comparison does not prove lateness caused a poor review or predict a pilot’s impact.

02 / WHERE TIME ACCUMULATES

The largest time gap appears after handover.

Late orders averaged 27.9 days from handover to delivery, compared with 8.0 days for on-time orders. Approval-to-handover was also longer: 5.6 versus 2.7 days.

Ask for tracking scans, failed-attempt records and route information before deciding what to change. The timestamp interval can include transport, sorting and waiting.

03 / WHERE TO INVESTIGATE

Volume and risk point to different priorities.

São Paulo has more late orders. But its 4.5% late rate is below the overall 6.8% rate. Rio de Janeiro’s rate is 12.1%, with nearly as many late orders from a much smaller order base.

Rio accounts for 12.8% of eligible orders but 22.9% of late orders. It is a sensible place to start a diagnostic sample; São Paulo remains important because of volume. These are unadjusted destination comparisons, not evidence that location itself causes delays.

FROM EVIDENCE TO A BUSINESS DECISION

Start with a focused investigation.
Design a better handoff.

01

Review late-order journeys in Rio.

Compare late and on-time orders from similar months, sellers and categories. Join carrier tracking events to locate repeated waiting points. Retain São Paulo on the high-volume watchlist.

02

Propose one shared overdue-order queue.

Give operations and support one record per order, showing the promise date, latest verified stage, days overdue and proposed owner. Missing or impossible dates should be flagged explicitly.

03

Test whether earlier communication helps.

Run a measured pilot with a suitable comparison group. Track customer contacts, review outcomes, delivery outcomes and handling time. Set targets after a baseline is agreed.

The proposed decision: approve a diagnostic review and define a small pilot. No savings, delivery improvement or operational rollout has been demonstrated by this portfolio project.

THE BUSINESS ANALYSIS WORK

A finding needs a usable requirement.

Keep the order whole.

Need: an analyst should not see three exceptions just because an order contains three items.

Acceptance: each eligible overdue order appears once, regardless of item count.

Make uncertainty visible.

Need: the team should know whether a parcel is waiting or a timestamp is missing.

Acceptance: unknown dates display “unknown”; impossible sequences are flagged and excluded from stage averages.

Keep comparisons fair.

Need: a large destination should not look unreliable solely because it has more orders.

Acceptance: show counts and rates together, updating both numerator and denominator when filtering.

Proposed requirements for a future solution. The interactive readout above demonstrates the count-versus-rate principle; it is not a live exception-management system.

What was excluded, and why?

99,441 source orders → exclude 2,963 non-delivered statuses and 8 delivered orders missing core dates → 96,470 eligible orders. The stage comparison additionally excludes 1,388 missing or inconsistent timestamp sequences.

Recent and non-delivered orders may have incomplete outcomes. The delivered-only rate does not describe eventual reliability for every purchase. Historical patterns should be rechecked with current operational data.

What can this dataset not tell us?

It cannot establish carrier fault, stockouts, warehouse performance, reasons for failed delivery, the effect of an intervention or financial savings. Destination and review comparisons are observational. Review timing and who chooses to respond can influence the results.
