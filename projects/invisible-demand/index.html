A customer asks for ten kilos. You can supply six. What happens to the four kilos your sales report never sees?

START HERE

Selling less doesn’t always mean customers wanted less.

Imagine a meat distributor deciding tomorrow’s purchases from yesterday’s sales. When stock runs out, sales stop recording what customers wanted. A request log could make that missing demand visible.

But meat has a limited selling window. Automatically buying more can turn missed sales into excess stock and waste. The useful question is:

When does recording unmet demand help a buyer—and when does the buying rule need to change?

This is a controlled simulation of a fictional distributor. It tests an idea; it does not report Unified Meat Packers’ stock, waste or purchasing performance.

THE EXPERIENCE BEHIND THE QUESTION

From sales reporting
to a better question.

My current CV lists Commercial Data Analyst & Sales Manager at Unified Meat Packers, September 2019–January 2023. It reports that Excel tracking tools and sales forecasting models supported a 35% increase in total revenue.

That experience inspired this case. The 35% is a CV-reported achievement, with no comparison period specified. It is not a finding from this simulation, and the fictional records were not designed to reproduce it.

THE ANSWER IN 30 SECONDS

More visibility did not automatically make the buying rule better.

Across 20 baseline runs, recording all unmet demand changed fulfilment from 99.60% to 99.61%, while modelled contribution fell by about KES 5,994 over the 180-day evaluation. With a smaller stock buffer, the average contribution change became slightly positive. The result depends on the policy and assumptions.

All figures are simulated. Contribution includes product margin, expiry losses and an assumed stock-holding charge; it is not net profit and excludes the cost of logging requests.

HOW THE EXPERIMENT WORKS

Change the information.
Keep the comparison fair.

01 / SALES ONLY

Record what left the shelf.

The buying rule learns from fulfilled kg. Unfilled requests do not enter its demand history.

02 / PARTIAL LOGGING

Record some missed demand.

The rule sees fulfilled kg plus approximately half of unfilled kg. This is an illustrative capture level, not measured staff behaviour.

03 / COMPLETE LOGGING

Record every requested kilo.

The rule sees fulfilled plus all unfilled kg. This idealised condition shows what fuller information can change.

Each policy receives the same customer demand and supplier-delay sequence within a run. All use a trailing 28-day average and the same buying rule. We test four fictional products across five operating scenarios and 20 random seeds.

Explain the terms without the jargon
Unmet demand
Requested quantity the distributor could not supply that day.
Fill rate
The percentage of requested kilos supplied. It does not count how many complete orders were delivered.
Stock buffer
Extra stock coverage in the buying target, beyond the planned wait for replenishment and next review.
Waste rate
Expired kilos divided by opening stock plus receipts during the evaluation.
Modelled contribution
Sales less the cost of sold goods, expired goods and an assumed daily stock-holding charge. Other operating costs are excluded.

01 / THE BASELINE RESULT

The extra buying had little demand left to satisfy.

The baseline already fulfils more than 99% of requested kg. Adding the missed requests to this rule raises stock and expiry slightly, while adding very little fulfilment. More information is not the problem; automatically translating it into more buying is what we are testing.

02 / TEST THE BOUNDARY

The same information can lead to a different result under a leaner policy.

With one less buffer day, full logging increases mean fulfilment from 97.81% to 98.00%. Its contribution is higher in 13 of 20 paired runs. The average increase is small and excludes staff time spent logging.

This does not establish a best policy. It shows why a real trial needs an agreed service requirement and a waste constraint before choosing how much stock to carry.

03 / TRY THE COMPARISON

Which trade-off would your stakeholder accept?

Choose an operating scenario. Compare service, waste and stock together; a single “better” number cannot choose the policy for you.

Precomputed simulation results, averaged across 20 runs. This control changes only this comparison. It does not issue purchase orders or recommend a food shelf life.

THE BUSINESS DECISION

Test the log.
Don’t automate the purchase yet.

01

Make missed requests visible.

Propose a small request log alongside existing sales records. Record requested kg, fulfilled kg, the shortfall and its reason. Audit completion and staff time so the information has a measurable cost.

02

Review shortages with stock age.

A buyer needs both the demand gap and the remaining selling window. More stock is only one possible response; investigate supply reliability and the buying rule before changing quantities.

03

Agree the guardrails before a trial.

Sales, purchasing and finance should agree an acceptable fill rate, waste constraint and comparison period. Measure service and contribution after logging costs. No target or return on investment is established by these fictional results.

TURN THE QUESTION INTO REQUIREMENTS

Capture the four kilos.
Keep the accounting honest.

One request, one record.

Acceptance: 10 kg requested and 6 kg supplied must reconcile to 4 kg unfilled. Duplicate entries are flagged.

A request is not revenue.

Acceptance: logging an unfilled request leaves invoice totals unchanged. Unknown quantities remain unknown.

A flag is not an order.

Acceptance: the log creates a buyer review item. Stock age and open purchase orders are visible before an approved purchase decision.

Proposed requirements for a fictional solution, not a workflow deployed at Unified Meat Packers.

SOURCES, METHODS & HONEST LIMITS

An established problem.
A decision worth testing.

Research already documents how stockouts hide demand. This project applies that established idea to a simple reporting decision and retains the unfavourable results. It does not claim a new forecasting method or a first-of-its-kind discovery.

Sachs & Minner’s censored-demand research informs the problem framing; the INSEAD publication summary on stockout timing motivates attention to what gets recorded. Our code does not implement those papers’ methods.

What this simulation cannot prove

The records are generated, and the outcomes follow chosen assumptions. Shelf lives and prices are illustrative. The model excludes substitution, backlogs, supplier capacity limits, customer switching, logging errors and most operating costs. It cannot establish actual employer performance, food safety, savings or an optimal purchasing policy.

Full logging assumes unfulfilled requests can be observed. In practice, silent walkaways may never appear in the log. The model also counts remaining stock without forecasting its future expiry; an age-aware buying rule is a possible extension, not an implemented feature.

My contribution and AI assistance

I supplied the experience context and directed the business-analysis and storytelling goals. AI assisted the research, simulation, calculations, validation and implementation. This is a learning project; no actual stakeholder interviews, employer dataset or implemented operational improvement is claimed.

THE TAKEAWAY

A better record is a starting point.
A better decision still needs a test.

My job as an analyst is to explain what the information changes—and what it doesn’t—so the team can choose its next step with the trade-off in view.
